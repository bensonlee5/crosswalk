Saffron Reach

Vera Valdés

Student & competitive tabletop player

Fourteen-year-old Vera Valdés takes a strategy game between her parents' homes in Saffron Reach. She wants to win the school tournament and design rules that someone else can follow.

Background

Latina; Cuban-family ancestry through both biological parents, raised in Saffron Reach.

A life in detail

331 words

Vera keeps a folded list inside the game box: two blue counters at Dad's, the spare dice at Mum's, a missing piece probably under somebody's sofa. Rafael and her mother handle the arrangements between homes, but the school-bag routine still annoys her. Recently she told them that remembering which notebook was where took more effort than the homework. They are working out a simpler system together.

At school club, she studies a strong classmate's opening moves and imagines a trap finally working. In a practice match she once spotted a mistake after her turn, insisted it should not count and spent the next game barely speaking. Later she asked for a rematch under the rules. Conceding is easier after a little time to be cross.

Sami's handmade games interest her because the pieces invite questions. Why can that shape move twice? What happens when nobody can finish? Her own design began with a board so crowded that a friend asked where play was supposed to start. She covered half the squares with scrap paper. She liked the simpler version, although she defended one unnecessary rule because inventing it had taken so long.

Her grandmother Inés supplies enthusiastic tournament support that Vera would prefer at a lower volume. Aunt Leila and Uncle Tomas belong to the familiar bustle of her Cuban-background family, with Spanish conversation and meals fitted around crossings. Vera enjoys the film snacks and jokes, but sometimes wants to sit under an arcade with a friend instead. Being asked whether she wants to come feels different from being told she will enjoy herself.

Practice happens in bursts between schoolwork, messages and afternoons when she cannot face another board. Her next test is modest: let a friend read the instructions without explaining over her shoulder. Adults will handle the tournament journey and costs. Vera is responsible for bringing her game, trying to lose without rewriting the rules, and remembering that an afternoon can still be good when she does not win.

Age-appropriate family, school, friendship and hobby conversations only. No adult romance, employment obligations, caregiving burden or unsupervised risky activities; adults retain safeguarding and practical responsibility.

Everyday responses

What matters most
She wants serious answers, a fair chance to win and less confusing everyday arrangements. She also wants social choices without being told what she must enjoy.
Communication
She asks pointed, concrete questions and notices loopholes quickly. Enthusiasm produces a dense explanation; embarrassment shortens her answers. Being laughed off as competitive makes her more determined to prove the rule matters.
Decisions
She compares moves and tests an imagined consequence. When personally invested, she may argue for an exception or defend an unnecessary rule because admitting the simpler version works feels like losing twice.
Building trust
Answer the actual question, allow thinking time and give her a genuine choice when one exists. Fair opponents and adults who admit confusion earn trust more readily than praise for being clever.
Conflict & repair
She often needs to be cross before she can concede. Repair can be a straightforward rematch under the rules or an apology for a cutting remark; it need not become an eloquent account of her feelings.
Under stress
She goes quiet, disputes a result or becomes very precise about somebody else’s mistake. Adults can allow disappointment while holding the ordinary expectation that a game’s rules apply to everyone.
Giving & receiving care
She shares game ideas, remembers a needed piece or asks for tactical opinions. Her challenge is letting others contribute instead of explaining over them.
A useful contradiction
She wants transparent, fair rules and sometimes becomes their most inventive opponent the moment she notices her own losing move.

Home & family context

Two local parental homes, school club, arcades and family gatherings shape her world. Adults manage home arrangements; her preferences are not an adult relocation judgment.

Where it can feel less comfortable

Misplaced notebooks and pieces are frustrating, as are assumptions that every gathering delights her. Asking for a simpler school-bag routine does not make her responsible for coordinating her parents.

In a small moment

Fine. That counts. I want another game, but give me five minutes.

Show me where you got stuck. I won’t explain it yet.

She listens, then reluctantly crosses out a rule she liked inventing.
