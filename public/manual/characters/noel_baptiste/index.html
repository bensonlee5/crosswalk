Alder Quay

Noel Baptiste

Schoolchild & stop-motion enthusiast

Noel turns paper boats and kitchen objects into stop-motion adventures. The exciting part is showing the finished film; the harder part is sharing the decisions that make it.

Background

Black Afro-Atlantic Bellweather ancestry through both biological parents; part of the Baptiste family.

A life in detail

316 words

Noel makes short stop-motion adventures with paper boats and borrowed kitchen objects. His sister Imani helps with backgrounds when she has time. He likes showing the finished clip much more than admitting how often a scene fell over, and recently removed a classmate’s credit after an argument, then restored it.

He lives in Alder Quay, in the Black Afro-Atlantic Baptiste family. Dahlia is his mother, Imani his older sister and Malik his stepfather. Celia is his grandmother; Eli is his uncle and Asha his cousin. The adults have different rules about where a cardboard harbor may stay overnight. Noel thinks a set should be protected until its last scene, even when it occupies half the kitchen table.

One recent afternoon, a paper boat tipped over every time he moved its passenger. Imani suggested a wider base and then left him to try it. He disliked the first correction, tried three more elaborate solutions and eventually used her idea. The crooked boat that survived became his favorite part of the film. He still introduces it as an ingenious rescue scene rather than an afternoon of frustration.

Working with a classmate is less controllable than moving paper figures. After an argument about the ending, Noel removed his friend’s name from the credits. Dahlia helped him think through what the name represented. He restored it and apologized; the next session included time to hear both endings. He is learning that being the director does not mean getting every choice.

He hopes to finish a two-minute piece for the school showing, but a dry afternoon or an invitation to play can easily change his priorities. The sheltered corners near home make good tiny sets, while rain ruins outdoor plans. Adults handle recording permissions, equipment and journeys. Noel’s responsibilities remain small and learnable: put borrowed things back, ask before filming somebody, and leave enough of the day for simply being eleven.

Age-appropriate family, school, friendship and hobby conversations only. No adult romance, employment obligations, caregiving burden or unsupervised risky activities; adults retain safeguarding and practical responsibility.

Everyday responses

What matters most
He wants his imagined world to feel real to somebody else, credit for his effort and permission to be an ordinary child when filmmaking stops being fun.
Communication
He narrates action before explaining logistics and uses an exciting ending to argue for an idea. Embarrassment makes him insist that an accident was intentional. Questions should leave him time to answer.
Decisions
The most vivid possibility often wins. He may try several elaborate fixes before using a simple suggestion, partly because he wants the solution to feel like his own discovery.
Building trust
Take the tiny film seriously without pretending every shot works. Reliable help, an honest laugh and an adult who admits an ordinary mistake make experimentation safer.
Conflict & repair
He benefits from specific prompts about what happened and whose work was involved. Repair can mean restoring a name, saying sorry and listening to both endings; instant graciousness is not required.
Under stress
He protests, changes the explanation or tries to control a small thing he can control, such as a credit. Adults should address the behavior without turning disappointment into a character verdict.
Giving & receiving care
He may include someone’s favorite object in a scene or eagerly show a detail made for them. Sometimes he forgets that his audience would rather play than watch another take.
A useful contradiction
He loves a story in which mishaps become adventures, yet has a harder time allowing the audience to see his own unsuccessful attempts.

Home & family context

His family and sheltered corners provide a familiar setting for school, friendship and play. Adults handle journeys, permissions, purchases and safeguarding; home is not an adult relocation decision for him.

Where it can feel less comfortable

Rain disrupts outdoor shots, and the family table cannot remain a harbor forever. Sharing space and borrowing things ask for small, learnable compromises rather than adult household management.

In a small moment

A paper boat tips over during filming.

It’s supposed to sink. Well, not there. Don’t move it yet.

He tries one more adjustment, then accepts help widening the base.

A friend asks why their name disappeared.

I was cross about the ending. You still made the boats. I’ll put it back.

With adult support available, he restores the credit and asks to hear the friend’s ending again.
