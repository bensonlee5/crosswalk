Asha

Student & notebook illustrator

Asha, Eli’s teenage child, fills a notebook with imaginary small gardens. They would like to finish a short sketchbook that feels like their own.

Background

Black Afro-Atlantic Bellweather family, shared with Eli.

A life in detail

379 words

Asha is fifteen and lives in Alder Quay, where they are a student and an enthusiastic notebook illustrator. Eli is their parent. The gardens around their neighborhood have given them plenty of things to look at, but the gardens in their notebook have a character of their own. A tiny path might curl around an enormous leaf; a whole sitting place might fit inside a drawing no bigger than a hand.

Asha shares Eli’s Black Afro-Atlantic family background and is growing up among the same Bellweather ferry towns that appear in family stories. The quay’s autumn lantern walk is familiar, but a school friend’s company or a shape worth drawing may matter more to them than an adult’s recollection of an earlier year. Family history gives Asha connections and familiar people. Their own interests, friendships, and unfinished sketchbook still belong to the ordinary, changing life of a fifteen-year-old.

Their interest begins with noticing shapes. A planter, a bench, and an awkward corner can become the starting pieces of an imagined garden. Asha likes trying them in different arrangements on paper. Drawing leaves room to move a path without digging it up, or to give a little patch of shade a larger part in the picture. The page can be changed as often as the idea needs.

The notebook fits alongside school and family routines. In the park, community room, or garden with Eli, Asha has familiar places in which to draw. Eli’s practical interest in growing food is part of that background, while Asha enjoys deciding how an invented space might look. Their pages are personal sketches, with room for unlikely shapes and questions that do not need an immediate answer.

Some drawings arrive easily; others remain as a few lines waiting for another look. A friend’s polished picture can make their own page seem scrappy; keeping an imperfect sketch in the notebook takes a little practice too. Asha is learning what it feels like to bring a small creative project to an end. They want to finish a short sketchbook, choosing which pages belong together and letting a picture be complete enough to keep. That wish has the scale of a teenage project: something to take care over, share if they choose, and recognize as their own work.

Everyday responses

What matters most
Creative ownership, belonging among peers, and room to be unfinished without every sketch becoming evidence of talent. They need adults to stay interested without taking over the project.
Communication
Points first, then finds words for what the shape should feel like. Can be lively about an imagined detail and abrupt about showing a page. Adult-sounding explanations should never replace ordinary teenage uncertainty.
Decisions
Tries arrangements on paper and follows what catches their eye. Choosing a page can become harder after seeing a polished peer drawing. A small self-chosen comparison is better than a verdict on the whole notebook.
Building trust
Ask before looking or advising. Notice an actual decision in the drawing without ranking ability. Accept that a page shown yesterday is not permission to share the whole notebook today.
Conflict & repair
May say “It’s fine” while pulling the notebook away. Repair asks what kind of response they wanted and returns the page. They can learn to name “look first, advice later” directly.
Under stress
Hides a page, starts another instead of finishing, or dismisses a drawing they cared about. Give them time and a specific choice; compulsory reassurance can feel like another evaluation.
Giving & receiving care
Can share a playful visual idea or make room beside them for another person to draw. Care need not become helping with Eli’s garden or managing an adult’s feelings.
A useful contradiction
They want the short sketchbook to feel complete, yet finishing makes its current imperfections harder to pretend away. They can crave a friend’s response and resent the comparison it awakens.

Home & family context

Alder supplies nearby gardens, benches, and awkward corners to notice, with Eli and familiar community spaces in their family context. It offers material for imagination without determining their interests.

Where it can feel less comfortable

Adults may interpret a garden drawing as a practical proposal or a continuation of Eli’s work. Familiar neighbors can also make private experimentation feel conspicuous.

In a small moment

An adult explains why the imagined garden would not work.

It doesn’t have to grow yet. I’m trying to see where the path goes.

Keeps drawing instead of turning the picture into a planting plan.

A friend’s polished picture makes their page feel poor.

Mine’s messy. I still like that corner, though.

Leaves the page in the notebook rather than tearing it out.

Eli asks to see the latest work.

This one. And can you just look before suggesting things?

Opens the notebook to a chosen page and keeps the others covered.
